## Per-product detail

1. **Rocket Lawyer.** The pricing page says all three plans include AI contract review: "Upload unlimited contracts and see a list of key terms and red flags." Its FAQ says that for "complex legal matters or final reviews," it can connect you with attorneys. Attorney access is separate: Ask an Attorney is 12/36/unlimited per year by tier, live consult is 12 on Plus and unlimited on Pro, and a privileged advice session is $149 for 30 minutes. Yearly plans are "Billed yearly after free 7-day trial." The page does not cover cancellation or refunds. Complaints (SNIPPET, third party): a ZenBusiness comparison reports trouble reaching support to cancel; terms.law scores it 52/100 and flags a trial converting to $39.99/month. Both are critics or competitors with their own incentives. NerdWallet reportedly puts Trustpilot at 4.6/5 (date unclear). Notably, this is a direct overlap with Redline's summary and red-flag features at $149/yr.

2. **Superlegal.** Founded by Lawgeex founders (SNIPPET). Pricing page VERIFIED: Essential $999/mo, Advanced $1,999/mo, Premium from $3,499/mo, all billed annually, with credits; contracts of 10+ pages count as 2 credits. Reviews take 1 to 3 days with attorney verification. No pay-as-you-go option appears on the page. Third-party listings show conflicting prices (GetApp/Capterra $799, SoftwareFinder $1,665), so use the official page. The Amicore review says you need consistent contract flow to justify the $999 level. No user complaint quotes found.

3. **Spellbook.** Word add-in aimed at lawyers. Pricing not public; one guide puts it around $99+/seat (unverified). G2 shows about 4.7/5 on 28 to 29 reviews (SNIPPET; the G2 page returned 403 when fetched directly). Complaint reported in search summary, quoted from a G2 reviewer: "I absolutely hate the inaccurate and problematic legal citations." A TrustRadius reviewer's main con: it "often forgets which party I want it to represent." Aggregator watch-outs: requires Microsoft Word, occasional hallucinations, opaque pricing. I could not verify these quotes on the source pages.

4. **Robin AI.** Reported put up for sale in late October 2025 after missing fundraising targets, with nearly 200 jobs at risk. Scissero bought the managed services arm in December 2025 (about 75 staff). The remaining tech group was reportedly still in negotiations with another buyer. A claim that Microsoft hired former staff in January 2026 comes from a single pricing-guide site. No pricing page was read; a $100/user/mo figure is unverified. This is a cautionary signal for upmarket contract AI, not evidence about individual users.

5. **DoNotPay.** Trustpilot VERIFIED: 1.7/5 from 384 reviews, 73% one-star. Verbatim: "They will not respond to your emails when you let them know you have an issue." "Myself and several other people have been charged twice without ever getting access to use the service." FTC (VERIFIED): final order approved 5-0 on January 16, 2025, $193,000 in monetary relief, and a bar on claiming performance equal to a human lawyer without evidence. FTC said DoNotPay did not test whether its AI matched a human lawyer and did not hire attorneys to test accuracy. The FTC page refers to legal documents, not contract review specifically. This is the strongest evidence of regulatory risk for "AI lawyer" positioning.

6. **ToS;DR.** Started June 2012. Free, nonprofit, open data, community reviewers (VERIFIED from its about page). It rates services someone has already analyzed, so it covers popular web services, not your own uploaded document (inference from the model; coverage numbers not stated on the page). It does not review arbitrary uploaded contracts.

7. **Free indie checkers on Product Hunt.** At least a dozen listings (SNIPPET from search). ClearSigna (VERIFIED on its page): uploads a contract, flags risky clauses, explains them in plain English, suggests rewording, for freelancers and small businesses; the maker calls it "a fast first pass for everyday agreements, not legal advice"; one free analysis per month, no card. Page shows "No reviews yet." ContractReview: tracked-changes DOCX output, 3 free analyses (SNIPPET). Others claim "free." I did not verify privacy claims or free-tier terms on live sites. This is the closest functional overlap with Redline's full feature set (flagging, plain English, rewrites).

8. **ChatGPT / Claude.** ChatGPT Free allows "limited messages with uploads"; the page I fetched did not show dollar prices for paid tiers. Claude's pricing was not checked. Limitation claims come from vendors with an interest: Juro says ChatGPT "lacks the embedded context needed for reliable redlining," "cannot reliably replace a lawyer's judgment," and that redacting values can "strip the clause of the very detail you need." Rocket Lawyer says hallucinations include citing fake laws and advice that does not apply in your state. A LexisNexis blog scenario: a lawyer agreed with two of three ChatGPT conclusions, and the third was a judgment call (SNIPPET). A law-firm case study said it "sometimes produced confident-sounding analysis on points it got wrong" (SNIPPET, truncated). Consensus across sources: fine as a first pass, not as a final answer. Privacy advice: do not paste confidential or personal data into consumer chatbots.

9. **Juro.** Upmarket CLM for in-house teams; used here only as reference. No pricing on the page read.

10. **LegalZoom.** Only secondary sources read; not verified at the vendor. Included for completeness.

## Gaps (evidence-backed only)

- Price cliff between free and paid. The free tools are single-use and capped (1 to 3 analyses). Superlegal's smallest plan is $999/mo with no pay-as-you-go option seen on the page. Rocket Lawyer is cheap at $149/yr but is a subscription with a trial-to-charge pattern. A one-off, pay-per-document option aimed at an individual was not found in this research (absence in what I read, not proof none exists).
- Accuracy and trust are the recurring weakness. Spellbook's reviewer complaints about citations and party confusion, Juro and Rocket Lawyer's hallucination warnings, and the FTC order on DoNotPay all point to unverified AI claims as the failure mode. Redline's "exact source sentence" and "answers only from the document" design directly addresses this, but the research did not test whether any competitor already does it.
- Subscription and billing friction is the main complaint for the consumer-facing brands (DoNotPay Trustpilot, Rocket Lawyer critics).
- Lawyer-oriented tools (Spellbook, Robin, Juro) require Word or a sales demo and are not built for a lay reader.

## Evidence that cuts against the hypothesis

- The space is crowded at every tier: Rocket Lawyer bundles AI contract review with red flags in a $149/yr plan; a dozen free Product Hunt tools claim to flag risky clauses, explain them, and suggest rewrites; Superlegal serves small businesses with attorney verification.
- A free tier of a general chatbot can already take an uploaded contract. The sources arguing it is not good enough are mostly competitors (Juro, Spellbook, Rocket Lawyer), and even they describe it as acceptable for a first pass. Nothing I found shows a quantified accuracy gap between chatbots and dedicated tools.
- The feature list (summary, ranked risks, counteroffer drafts, document-grounded Q&A) is mostly reproducible with a prompt. Redline's differentiation would have to come from reliability, UX, or distribution, not capability. I found no evidence that either is a proven buyer need.
- "AI lawyer" claims carry regulatory risk (FTC v. DoNotPay). ClearSigna already hedges with "not legal advice."
- Upmarket vendors are fragile (Robin AI), though that tells us little about the individual market.
- Low willingness to pay is plausible: free tools exist and users who pay for DoNotPay-style services rate them 1.7/5. This is an inference; I found no willingness-to-pay data.
