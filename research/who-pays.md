# Redline: who would pay?

Research budget used: 11 web searches (cap 12), 11 page-read attempts (cap 15). 5 of the 11 reads succeeded. The others failed with ECONNREFUSED, 403, or an unreadable PDF.

**Verification caveat.** Only three sources were read directly:
- the LSC press release
- the Freelancers Union blog
- the LawNext Law Insider article

Everything else comes from the web-search tool's summaries of result pages, and I did not open those pages. Treat those figures as "reported by search summary, unverified at the page". Many are vendor marketing. Rows are marked **[read]** or **[snippet]**.

Pain and willingness to pay (WTP) are kept separate throughout. Most of what I found is pain. Direct WTP evidence is thin.

## Findings detail

1. **Lawyer review is priced at a few hundred dollars per contract.** ContractsCounsel reports an average flat fee of about $400 for freelance contract review ($360 for 1-5 pages, $400 for 6-10 pages) and about $390 on another page. Other guides cite $200-$600 and $450-$3,000. Hourly: $200-$350 for freelance specialists. [snippet, marketplace and vendor sources.] https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/freelance-contract-review-cost, https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/freelance-contract-cost

2. **The cost barrier is real for low-income people, but it is not the same population as Redline's buyers.** LSC press release: low-income Americans received no or inadequate legal help for a "staggering 92% of all the civil legal problems that impacted them substantially." "74% of low-income households experiencing at least one in the past year." Belief that "costs would preclude them from receiving legal help was the most common reason" for not seeking help. The most common problem types are "health care, housing, consumer issues and income maintenance." A secondary summary gives 46% citing cost, which the press release itself does not state. **[read]** https://www.lsc.gov/press-release/low-income-americans-face-immense-justice-gap-according-new-legal-services-corporation-report

3. **Freelancers skip lawyers.** Freelancers Union blog: "Too many freelancers avoid this mystery by not hiring a lawyer even when they need one." Also: "The costs can go from almost free (pro bono) to several hundred dollars per hour," and "In my practice and many others the first discussion is free." No stats on this page. **[read]** https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2017/05/24/a-freelancers-guide-to-hiring-a-lawyer-2/

4. **Freelancers rarely use written contracts, so there may be nothing to review.** A 2015 Freelancers Union survey of 5,358 freelancers found 28% regularly work under written contract, 42% always use a verbal agreement, and nonpayment removed 13% of average income. [snippet; I could not read the original PDF.] https://onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf

5. **Small business owners cite cost, but the surveys come from vendors.** Rocket Lawyer (2010) and Avvo (2016) report 51% saying lawyers are too expensive. LegalShield (2013): 54% of owners with a significant legal event did not use an attorney; of those, 40% cited cost and 57% thought they could handle it themselves. Contract review was among the most-sought reasons for legal help. [snippet, vendors that sell legal plans, 10+ years old.] https://www.legalshield.com/newsroom/legal-pitfalls-dent-small-business-owners-bottom-line

6. **Cost is not the main driver of avoidance, even in Clio's own data.** Clio 2018: 31% agree the legal system costs too much, 35% say the benefit doesn't justify cost, 28% cite not knowing the final cost. Clio's blog says cost isn't the determining factor in whether to use a lawyer, and among people who avoided hiring, cost-related factors had an impact of 0-5%. [snippet] https://www.clio.com/blog/surprising-finding-legal-trends/

7. **Willingness to pay for AI contract review: price points exist, demand measurement does not.** Competing tools price at a free tier (about 3 reviews/month), $9.99/month or $79/year, about $19-30/month, or about $3 per contract. Law Insider bundles AI tools into plans "starting at $29 per month for individuals," with a free version planned for its 1.2 million registered users. None of these pages measure what people actually pay. **[read for Law Insider]**, [snippet for the rest]. https://www.lawnext.com/2025/03/law-insider-announces-low-cost-and-free-ai-powered-contract-tools-for-1-2-million-legal-users.html, https://www.capterra.com/p/10049953/ContractReview/

8. **Employee and tenant review is priced on the same scale as freelance review.** US employment flat fees listed: $175 (basic offer letter), $300 (written evaluation), $400 (physician agreement, 30 pages), $1,000-$1,500 (CA employment contracts). Lease review averages $610 (ContractsCounsel) and $500-$1,000 (Gerald, a promotional source). [snippet] https://terms.law/Employment-Contract-Review-Hub/, https://www.contractscounsel.com/p/10209/review-employment-offer-letter-in-new-york

## Price anchors

What lawyers charge:
- **Average US lawyer hourly rate:** about $362 (Clio rates page, Oct 2026, per search summary). I could not open the page (403). https://clio.com/resources/legal-trends/compare-lawyer-rates/
- **Freelance contract review, flat fee:** about $360-$400 (ContractsCounsel).
- **Creator brand deal review:** $300-$500, up to $2,000 for an entertainment lawyer.
- **Employee contract review:** $175-$1,500.
- **Lease review:** $500-$1,000 flat, or $610 on ContractsCounsel.

What people call too expensive or not worth it (all [snippet]):
- A rule of thumb in one guide is to reconsider if review cost exceeds about 3-5% of contract value, so about $250 on a $5,000 contract.
- Another source says short standard agreements under about 10 pages, or creator deals under about $5,000, can be reviewed yourself.
- A forum user paid about $300-$400 for a short contract on a $100-$200 gig and implied it was not worth it.
- Competitor price points are $3-$30 per contract or month. That is the likely price range Redline competes in.

## Evidence that cuts against the hypothesis

- **Cost is not the main reason people skip lawyers (finding 6).** Clio's own data says cost isn't the determining factor. People may skip review because they don't think they need it, not because of price.
- **Pain does not equal WTP.** Freelancers mostly don't use written contracts (28%). The 92% LSC figure covers low-income people with mostly housing, health and benefits problems, not contract review. Their ability to pay for any tool is the weakest.
- **The thresholds are high-stakes only.** Advice across sources is to do-it-yourself for contracts under about $5,000, under about 10 pages, or with standard language. That points to occasional purchase, not subscription.
- **Frequency looks low.** Leases, offer letters and brand deals are signed rarely, which supports per-document pricing at best. I found no usage-frequency data.
- **Free alternatives are already common.** Many AI contract tools have free tiers, and Law Insider plans a free version for 1.2 million users. General chatbots are also an alternative that these sources do not price.
- **The cost surveys come from vendors who sell legal products** (Rocket Lawyer, Avvo, LegalShield) and are 10+ years old.
- **Most of the lawyer price data is marketing from the sellers.**

## What I could not find

- Any direct WTP statement ("I would pay $X for this") or a stated-preference survey for AI contract review.
- Reddit or forum threads with price discussion. My one forum-style search returned no Reddit results. Metafilter (403) and ContractsCounsel (ECONNREFUSED) did not load.
- Verified figures from the Clio Legal Trends consumer section, Upwork, MBO Partners or Freelancers Union beyond search summaries. The Freelancers Union PDF was unreadable.
- Any data on musicians or early-stage founders.
- Segment sizes, repeat-purchase frequency or churn data.
- Independent evidence for creators (only vendor and law-firm claims).
- Recent (2024-2026) US small-business cost-avoidance surveys.
- Evidence on whether people trust AI enough to use it instead of a lawyer for high-stakes documents.
